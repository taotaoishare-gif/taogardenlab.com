## 01 Observe

Firecrackers first, before anything is visible, and everyone on the street
turns the same way at the same moment.

Then the figures — tall painted god-generals carried on frames by people
inside them, moving with a heavy sway because of the weight. Drums. Smoke that
smells of gunpowder and incense at once. The procession takes the whole width
of the road and traffic simply stops; there is no negotiation about it.

Old people at the front, phones held up at the back. Both.

What struck me is that nobody chose to attend. The parade arrives and takes
the street's attention the way weather does.

## 02 Contextualize

游神 — "the gods on tour" — is a Lunar New Year practice across Fujian and
parts of Taiwan and Southeast Asia, in which village deities are carried in
procession around a territory. The route matters: it traces the boundary of
the community the god is responsible for.

The practice is organised by village associations, funded by household
contributions, and was heavily suppressed during the Cultural Revolution
before a substantial revival from the 1980s. In recent years it has also
become a social-media subject, which has changed both the costumes and the
crowds.

## 03 Interpret

What problem does this solve?

A community needs occasions when everyone is doing the same thing. Not
believing the same thing — doing. The parade produces **synchronised
attention** without requiring anyone to agree about the metaphysics; you can
be devout, sceptical, or twelve years old and filming, and you are still
turning your head at the same moment as your neighbour.

It also produces a shared calendar. The event is annual, territorial, and not
optional, which makes it a timekeeping device as much as a religious one.

## 04 Translate

This is the entry that most resists translation, and that is why it is here.

Everything I build is single-user, private, and chosen. A person opens a
browser tab alone. The parade is public, collective, and imposed — and its
force appears to come precisely from those three properties.

The translatable fragment might be **imposition**: an environment that acts on
a schedule of its own rather than waiting to be summoned. A room that changes
at dusk whether or not anyone asked. That is a small piece of a large thing,
and I should not pretend it is the same thing.

## 05 Question

The sharpest version of my worry lives here.

This is a living religious practice belonging to specific villages. It is not
a source of visual motifs, and the god-generals are not characters. Extracting
"synchronised attention" as a design principle means taking the mechanism and
discarding the god — and the whole reason the mechanism works may be that the
people in the street are not treating it as a mechanism.

There is also a real difference between studying this as someone with family
ties to Fujian and studying it as a visitor with a camera, and I should be
explicit about which I am in any given entry, rather than letting the
ambiguity do flattering work.

I am fairly confident that "an environment that acts on its own schedule" is a
legitimate thing to take from this. I am not confident that the parade is where
I should say I took it from.
