## 01 Observe

A banana leaf is enormous, flat, and slightly stiff — a drum head the size of
a person. Rain on it is not a hiss, it is discrete: individual impacts, each
one audible.

Sitting under a covered walk while it rained, I could hear how hard the rain
was without looking at it. When the wind moved the leaf, the pitch changed.

The plant is doing nothing. It is being played.

## 02 Contextualize

雨打芭蕉 — rain striking the plantain — is a fixed motif in classical Chinese
poetry (Li Qingzhao among many others) and the title of a well-known
Cantonese instrumental piece.

In the poetic use it is almost always nocturnal and almost always about a
person indoors who cannot sleep, or who is waiting for someone, or who is far
from home. The rain is not the subject; the listening is.

Worth noting that the plant is grown for fruit and shade, and its acoustic
role is a by-product that culture picked up and elaborated.

## 03 Interpret

The problem: **the experience of waiting is intolerable without something to
attend to, and most things to attend to end the waiting.**

Rain on a leaf is a stream of events with no information in it. It cannot be
finished, it does not develop, and nothing is asked of the listener. That
combination gives attention somewhere to rest that does not consume the state
it is resting in.

The motif's persistence for a thousand years suggests this is a solved
problem, and that the solution is: sound that is eventful but not meaningful.

## 04 Translate

I built a rain-curtain piece from this. The specific
thing I took was the discreteness — individual drops rather than an ambient
wash — because the wash is what most rain-sound applications produce, and the
wash is the wrong thing. It is texture, and texture is not events.

Where I departed from the source, and should be honest about it: in the real
case, the rain is indifferent to you. In mine, the rain responds to your hand,
which makes it a different object. I gained interactivity and lost
indifference, and indifference may have been the load-bearing property.

## 05 Question

Whether making it responsive destroys it.

The poems are about a person who cannot influence the rain. That powerlessness
is the situation the motif exists to make bearable. A rain curtain that breaks
when you sweep your hand is a rain that obeys you — which is a fantasy of
control, and arguably the exact opposite of what the tradition is doing.

I do not think this invalidates the piece, but it means I should stop citing
the poems as though the piece were continuous with them. It is a response to
them, and possibly a contradiction of them.

The version I have not built is a rain that does not respond at all, and the
uncomfortable possibility is that it would work better.
