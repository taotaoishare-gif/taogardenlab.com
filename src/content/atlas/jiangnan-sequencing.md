## 01 Observe

The garden is smaller than the time it takes to walk it. You come through a
door that is too narrow for two people. There is a wall immediately in front
of you, close enough to touch, so the only thing you can do is turn. Around
the corner the path is covered and dark, and then it opens onto water, and the
opening is sudden in a way that feels arranged rather than found.

You cannot see across it. Something is always in the way — a rock, a wall, a
covered walk that folds back on itself. Twice I walked past a courtyard I had
already been in without recognising it.

The paving underfoot changes. Pebble, then flagstone, then pebble again, and
the change happens where the view changes.

## 02 Contextualize

These gardens were built by scholar-officials of the Ming and Qing, mostly on
small urban plots — a few mu, hemmed in by city. Their owners were frequently
men who had left, or been removed from, official posts. The garden was a
retreat that had to be constructed inside a city rather than found outside it,
which is a constraint that produces a particular kind of design: everything
has to be made to feel larger and slower than it is.

The vocabulary is well documented in the Ming manual 《園冶》 (Ji Cheng, 1631)
and in Chen Congzhou's 20th-century writing — 曲折 (winding), 障景 (blocked
view), 步移景異 (the view shifts as you step).

## 03 Interpret

What human problem does this solve?

Not beauty. A small plot could be made beautiful in a single glance. The
sequencing solves a problem about **duration**: how to make a short distance
occupy a long time, and how to make a person who is agitated arrive somewhere
slower than they left.

The mechanism appears to be denial of view. You cannot see where you are
going, so you cannot plan the route, so you cannot execute it efficiently.
Efficiency is unavailable. The walls are not decorative; they are what makes
hurrying impossible.

## 04 Translate

The translatable principle is not the shape of the openings, the rockery, or
the plants. It is: **withhold the overview, and pace follows.**

That maps badly onto screens, which are overview machines. A screen shows you
everything at once and lets you skip. The nearest honest analogue is not a
visual style but a structural refusal — no map, no progress bar, no scrubber,
no way to see how much is left.

I once tried the shape rather than the principle — a lattice window, rebuilt
on a screen — and it did not work, which is the most useful result I have had
from this entry.

## 05 Question

The risk here is specific: it is very easy to take "Chinese garden" as a
supply of shapes — moon gates, lattice, rockery — and produce an aesthetic
while discarding the argument. The moon gate is the most photographed and
least important part of the system.

There is a second risk I am less sure about. These gardens were private
retreats built by a wealthy, educated, male elite, for whom slowness was
available because other people were working. When I extract "slowness" as a
design principle for people who are exhausted rather than at leisure, am I
translating a practice, or am I selling the aesthetic of a privilege while
leaving its material conditions untouched?

I do not have an answer. I do not think the question invalidates the work,
but I think a version of this research that never asks it would be a worse
version.
