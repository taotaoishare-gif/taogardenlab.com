## 01 Observe

A lattice window in a white wall. Behind it: three stalks of bamboo and a
piece of wall. That is all. The window itself is worked stone or tile,
octagonal, with a tracery that took someone a long time.

The disproportion is the thing I keep coming back to. Enormous care in the
frame, nothing much in the view.

Standing at it, I looked at the bamboo for longer than I would have looked at
bamboo.

## 02 Contextualize

漏窗 (leaking window) and 框景 (framed view) are standard terms; 借景
(borrowed scenery) — incorporating a distant pagoda or hill that the garden
does not own — is named as a principle in 《園冶》, which treats it as the
highest skill in garden-making.

Borrowing has an economic dimension worth noting: it lets a small, cheap plot
lay claim to a mountain. The technique is partly a technique of poverty.

## 03 Interpret

The human problem: **ordinary things stop registering.** Habituation is
adaptive and it is also why you can walk past the same tree for years without
seeing it.

Framing appears to be a habituation-defeating device. It costs the viewer
nothing and changes nothing about the object — the bamboo is the same bamboo.
What changes is the assertion made by the frame: *this was worth building a
window for.* The elaboration of the frame is a claim about the value of the
content, and the viewer partly believes it.

This is close to what a gallery does with a wall label, and close to what a
poem does with a line break.

## 04 Translate

Can this move into a responsive environment? A frame is cheap, static, and
requires no sensing at all, which makes it an awkward fit for the research
question — it is a counter-example to the assumption that responsiveness is
what makes an environment participate in your attention. A wall with a hole in
it is not responsive and works fine.

The honest translation might be a caution rather than a feature: before adding
sensing, check whether a frame would do.

## 05 Question

Whether I can separate the frame from the wall. In the garden, the window is
an exception in a large expanse of blank white plaster — the frame works
because almost everything else is denied. On a screen, there is no wall; the
frame is the whole surface. I may be extracting a figure from a ground and
keeping the wrong one.

And: 借景 borrows a real hill that really exists at a real distance. Borrowing
a rendered hill borrows nothing. If the technique's force comes from the fact
that the pagoda is genuinely out there and genuinely not yours, a simulation
cannot inherit it, and calling it 借景 would be a decorative use of a term that
means something specific.
