## 01 Question

Most interfaces that want to slow you down tell you to slow down. They count
your breaths, or show you a circle to breathe with, and then measure how well
you did.

I wanted to know if a system could slow a person without instructing them —
by being a material that behaves differently at different speeds, the way
water does.

## 02 Context

雨打芭蕉 — "rain beating on banana leaves" — is a set phrase in Chinese poetry
and a guqin piece. It names a sound that is specifically about waiting: the
listener is indoors, the rain is outside, nothing is happening and the not-
happening is the subject.

The question came out of what failed in
[Computational Framing](/experiments/computational-framing/): the frame alone
did not hold anyone. Material behaviour might.

## 03 What I built

Strands of rain hang from the eaves. Each drop is a character from a poem.
A hand sweeping across the screen catches the strands; they stretch, and if
you are moving fast enough they break, and the characters fall and land on
banana leaves below, raising ripples and a note.

Speed changes what the material is:

- **Below the break threshold** (about 420 px/s) the strands only bend and
  give a harmonic — you have brushed the curtain, not broken it.
- **Above it**, the strand breaks and drops fall.
- **Sweeping fast and repeatedly** turns the note generator into a glissando:
  the system detects three or more strokes in a short window and runs a
  continuous scalar run along the direction of travel instead of sounding
  discrete notes.
- **Faster still**, the audio degrades on purpose to a flat water tick, because
  the note generator cannot keep up and I would rather it become plain water
  than become noise.

The melody is generated by a pentatonic Markov walk that prefers stepwise
motion and lands on the tonic or dominant at phrase ends, with a rest inserted
after — so a slow hand produces something that sounds like phrasing.

**Version 2** added a meditation mode, where the rain's speed and density are
driven by a breath value fused from three sources: a tidal 4-2-6 guide
(inhale-hold-exhale), holding the trackpad, and — if the camera is on — the
height of your palm rising and falling. Exhaling triggers a breeze across the
leaves and a low chime.

## 04 Why I designed it this way

The key decision was to put the slowness **in the material rather than in the
instruction**. Nothing on screen asks you to slow down. It is simply the case
that a fast hand gets water ticks and a slow hand gets music. I wanted to see
whether people would find the slow behaviour on their own.

I rejected a breathing circle, a countdown, a session timer visible during
use, and any post-session summary of how calm you were. A calmness score would
have made the piece into a test.

The interface hides itself: the control bar only appears when the pointer
approaches the bottom of the screen. There is a sleep timer (15/30/60 minutes)
that fades the last 45 seconds into a still night page, so the piece has an
ending it can reach without anyone deciding to stop.

## 05 What I observed

**Personal observation.** I found the slow behaviour by accident, days after
building the fast behaviour deliberately. That is weak evidence that it is
discoverable, and strong evidence that I am not a good test subject for my own
threshold values.

**Observation from other people: none recorded.** No structured feedback has
been collected.

**Evaluation: not yet conducted.** There is no measurement of breathing here at
all. The breath value in meditation mode is *driven by* the user; it is not
read from them. Palm height is not respiration. I want to be precise about
this because it would be easy to describe this piece in a way that implies
physiological sensing, and there is none.

## 06 What did not work

- **The tab-in-background crash.** In a hidden tab, `innerWidth` reports 0 and
  the animation frame loop freezes. Zero-width geometry produced `NaN`, `NaN`
  reached an AudioParam, and the exception took down the whole start routine.
  Every resize, spatial-audio and note-playing path now guards against a zero
  viewport. This cost me most of a day, and it was made much worse by the fact
  that exceptions thrown inside an event listener do not propagate to whatever
  dispatched the event — the failure was silent until I attached a window error
  listener.
- **The first melodic engine had no rests.** It produced an unbroken stream of
  correct-sounding notes, which is exhausting. Phrase endings and breath gaps
  had to be built explicitly; they do not emerge from a Markov chain that only
  knows about pitch.
- **Fast sweeping originally produced audio garbage** — dozens of overlapping
  voices. The glissando detection and the water-tick degradation are both
  patches over that failure, and I think the degradation is the better idea of
  the two.
- **"Brushing" versus "breaking" is a threshold, and thresholds are personal.**
  420 px/s is my hand. I have no idea whether it is anyone else's.

## 07 What comes next

The honest gap: I have built two pieces that claim to be about pace and have
never once measured anybody's pace. The next thing worth doing is not another
piece. It is a way of finding out whether a slow hand corresponds to anything
at all in the person attached to it.

Open question: whether the fortune-slip and message-in-the-hollow features I
added in v2 belong here. They give the piece a reason to be returned to, which
I wanted, but "a reason to return" is uncomfortably close to a retention
mechanic, and I have not decided if that is a real distinction or one I am
making because I like the feature.

## Reflection

This is the first piece where the material argued back. I set a break
threshold, and the threshold turned the same gesture into two different
gestures depending on how much of a hurry I was in — and I could feel the
difference in my arm before I noticed it on screen.

I do not know how to write about that without overclaiming. It is one person
noticing a threshold she set herself. But it is also the reason I kept going.
