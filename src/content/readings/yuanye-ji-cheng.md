The oldest surviving Chinese treatise on garden-making, written by a working
garden designer in the late Ming. It reads less like a manual than like a
practitioner explaining why manuals do not work.

The organising idea is 因借 — roughly, *follow the site and borrow from
beyond it*. The first half means the design comes out of the particular
ground: its slope, its existing trees, what is already there. The second means
the garden should annex things it does not own — a hill on the horizon, a
neighbour's pagoda, the moon.

Ji Cheng is explicit that there are no fixed formulas, and repeatedly declines
to give the kind of prescription his readers presumably wanted.

## What is useful to me

The refusal is the useful part.

I have been treating classical garden theory as a source of principles I can
extract and apply — sequencing, framing, borrowing. This book says that the
principles are not applicable in that way, because the whole method is
site-specific by definition. There is no general garden.

That is a direct criticism of what I do. I build screen pieces that have no
site. A browser tab is the same everywhere, which by Ji Cheng's account makes
it precisely the condition under which none of this transfers.

## Where I want to argue back

I think there is a site, and I have been ignoring it: the person's room. The
actual conditions of use — a laptop on a table at 11pm, a phone on a train,
light from a window behind the screen — are as specific as any slope or
existing tree, and I have designed for none of them.

因借 in that reading would mean: borrow the room. The window behind the screen
is a borrowed view already there. I have never once used it.

This is the most concrete idea I have taken from anything I have read this
year and I have not acted on it.

## Caution

Reading a 17th-century text in translation, in a modern edition, with modern
commentary, and taking a single phrase as a design principle is exactly the
extraction move I keep warning myself about in the atlas. 因借 is embedded in a
whole practice — literati culture, specific plants, specific labour
arrangements — and the two-character phrase travels much more easily than the
practice does. Easy travel is a warning sign, not a discovery.
