Augé distinguishes anthropological *places* — spaces that carry identity,
relations and history — from *non-places*: airports, motorways, chain hotels,
waiting areas. Non-places are not empty or unpleasant. They are functional,
often comfortable, and they relate to their occupants through instructions and
contracts rather than through history. You pass through them as a passenger,
identified by a ticket.

His point is not that non-places are bad. It is that supermodernity produces
them in enormous quantity, and that a life spent largely in them is a life with
an unusual relation to belonging.

## Why I keep coming back to it

Because I think a browser tab is a non-place by every criterion he gives, and
that includes mine.

My pieces are entered by a URL, identified by no history, relate to the user
through an instruction ("cup your hands"), and retain nothing. Close the tab
and there is no trace that you were there. That is the definition.

This is awkward, because the pieces are attempts to build the opposite —
something like a place, with duration and return.

## Where I part company

Augé writes in 1992, and I think the frame has aged in one specific way: he
treats the non-place as a location you travel through. The current version is
not travelled through, it is inhabited continuously, and it travels with you.
A phone is a non-place you never leave.

If that is right, the interesting problem is not how to avoid non-places. It is
whether a non-place can accumulate history — whether repetition alone can turn
one into a place.

## The test this gives me

A real test I could run and have not: does anyone come back?

Not "how many people" — I do not want that number and will not collect it.
But there is a difference in kind between a piece someone opens once and a
piece someone opens again on a different day, and it is the difference between
a place and a corridor. Every piece I have built is designed for a single
visit. The poster in [Cosmic Pottery](/experiments/cosmic-pottery) is the only
object that leaves the tab, and it leaves as an image, not as a reason to
return.

I notice I am uneasy here, because "designing for return" is a sentence that
belongs equally to Augé and to growth teams, and the vocabulary does not
distinguish them. I think the difference is whether the return is *invited* or
*engineered* — but see the note on [Rosa](/notes/rosa-resonance), where the
same distinction is doing a suspicious amount of work.
